% ============================================================ ABSTRACT (roman numbering starts here)
\chapter*{ABSTRACT}
\addtocline{\itshape}{Abstract}
When a company wants to bid for a government or corporate contract, it first has to read the tender
document, called a Request for Proposal (RFP). These documents often run to 50 to 100 pages, and the
details that matter (who is asking, what work is wanted, who is allowed to bid, deadlines, payment
terms, penalties) are scattered across them. RFPs are also confidential, so uploading them to an
online AI service is often not allowed. Many tenders are written in languages other than English. We have chosen to include German translation in Klaro as of now.

Klaro is a desktop application that reads an RFP and writes a short summary under nine headings
entirely on the user's own laptop. Nothing is sent over the internet. It uses a small AI language
model (Qwen3.5 with 4 billion parameters) that runs locally. A German RFP is first translated to
English on the laptop, and the finished summary can be shown in German. Every point shows the page it
came from. Clicking it opens the original PDF and highlights the passage it most likely came from.
The dates in a summary can be saved as a calendar file.

We compared three small models and chose Qwen3.5. Full runs on English RFPs of 54 and 84 pages took
22 to 25 minutes, and runs on German RFPs of 24 and 31 pages took 9 to 11 minutes. We also found a
fault: with the default memory setting, the summary step saw only about 12\% of the facts (2,050 of
17,496 tokens in one test), and the code filled the gaps by copying facts. Raising the setting cut the
average summary of 11 documents from 4,565 to 545 words, but made each run about three times
slower. On one German run it also cut the share of facts used from 98\% to 65\%. We built a set of 32 draft summaries (written by an AI and checked by machine, not yet by
a human) and a LoRA fine-tuning kit. The kit has not been trained on the real 4-billion-parameter model.

\vspace{6pt}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} tender documents, RFP, offline AI, small language model, document
summary, machine translation, German, privacy.

\chapter{INTRODUCTION}\nonum

\section{General}
A Request for Proposal (RFP) is a document an organization publishes when it wants companies to bid
for a piece of work. Before a bid team can decide whether to respond, someone has to read the whole
RFP and note who issued it, what work is required, who can bid, the deadlines, payment terms,
contract length, and special conditions such as penalties. These points are spread across many pages.

AI chatbots can summarize long documents, but that means uploading the document to someone else's
servers. Bid teams usually cannot do that with confidential tenders. Klaro takes a different
approach: the AI model runs on the user's own laptop, so the document never leaves it
(Figure~\ref{fig:overview}). Many tenders in Europe are in German, so Klaro also reads German RFPs.

\section{Problem Statement}
Given a tender document (RFP) as a PDF in English or German, produce a short summary under nine fixed
headings, where every point shows which page it came from. It must run on an ordinary laptop, use only
an AI model that runs on that laptop, and never send any part of the document over the internet. A
non-technical reader must be able to check each point against the original, and when something goes
wrong the app must say so clearly instead of showing a half-finished result.

\section{Objectives}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Build a step-by-step process that turns an RFP PDF into a page-referenced summary.
\item Pick the most suitable small AI model by testing several on the same task.
\item Measure the quality of the summaries and record the weak points honestly.
\item Package everything as a desktop app with progress updates and a side-by-side view of the original.
\item Accept German RFPs, translate them on the laptop, and show where each point came from in the PDF.
\item Prepare a reference dataset and a fine-tuning kit for improving the summary step.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope of the Present Work}
Klaro handles English and German RFPs in text-based PDF form and one feature, the executive summary.
Scanned files, Word files, other languages, requirement checklists, proposal drafts and search over
past proposals are left for later (Chapter~7).

% ------------------------------------------------------------ CHAPTER 2
\chapter{BACKGROUND STUDY}\nonum

\section{General}
Computers have been used to analyze tender documents since before today's AI chatbots. Older methods
counted important words and picked out the most important sentences. Newer methods use AI models
that can read long text. We studied papers close to our problem, plus background work on the kind of
AI model and translation system we use.

\section{Earlier Work on Tender Documents}
Beason et al.~\cite{beason2021} processed a large set of U.S.\ Department of Defense and Homeland
Security RFPs to spot technology trends. They found that RFPs follow no common layout and contain a
lot of noise (contents pages, boilerplate, legal text), which has to be cleaned out first. They also
found that exact, structured facts are more useful to readers than a free-flowing summary. Klaro
follows both points: it skips contents pages and produces fixed headings.

Trappey et al.~\cite{trappey2022} summarized more than 1,300 tenders for power transformers. They
found key terms by counting words (TF-IDF), grouped sentences by topic, and picked the highest-ranked
sentences with TextRank. Keeping the top 35\% of sentences preserved about 80\% of the important
requirements and cut reading time to about 29\%. We took two ideas from this: summarize topic by
topic rather than all at once, and aim for a real reduction in length. Knisely and
Pavliscsak~\cite{knisely2023} sorted research proposals into themes. A small number of proposals
were labelled by experts, and those labels guided the sorting of the rest, which worked much better
than sorting with no human input. This is why we built a set of reference summaries (Chapter~5).

\section{Reading Very Long Documents}
Standard AI models can only look at a limited amount of text at once, and the cost grows quickly as
the text gets longer \cite{vaswani2017}. Longformer \cite{beltagy2020} reduces this cost so a model
can read thousands of words in one go. Douzon et al.~\cite{douzon2023} also used the page layout of
business documents, so that a table value stays linked to its heading. A small model on a laptop
cannot read an 84-page RFP in one go, so Klaro cuts it into pieces, keeps page numbers on every piece
and combines the results afterwards.

\section{Small AI Models and Made-Up Answers}
Language models sometimes state things that are not in the text they were given
\cite{ji2023hallucination,maynez2020}, and they pay less attention to the middle of very long inputs
\cite{liu2024lost}. One remedy is to first look up the relevant passages and give only those to the
model (retrieval-augmented generation, \cite{lewis2020rag}). Klaro's remedy is to make the model
cite a page for every point and then check each point against the collected facts. A model can be
specialized for a task with light-weight fine-tuning such as LoRA \cite{hu2022lora}, which trains a
small add-on instead of the whole model.

\section{Machine Translation}
Machine translation turns text from one language into another with a trained model. OPUS-MT
\cite{tiedemann2020opusmt} is a set of small, open translation models, one per language direction.
They are small enough to run on a laptop without internet, which is why we use them. We found no
earlier work that combines offline translation, a small summary model and page-level checking for
tenders on a laptop. The papers above handle English documents only, or need powerful hardware.

\chapter{DETAILED DESIGN}\nonum

\section{How the Parts Fit Together}
The user works in a desktop app. When the app opens, it quietly starts a local server. When the user
adds an RFP, the server starts the analysis as a separate background job, so the app stays responsive
and the job can be cancelled at any time. Only one analysis runs at a time, because the laptop runs a
single AI model; others wait in a queue. The job saves everything it produces in its own folder. We
did not use a workflow engine such as LangGraph \cite{langgraph}: the steps are a fixed sequence, and
a few lines of Python run them.

\section{The Document Processing Pipeline}
Every analysis goes through the seven steps in Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}. Two of them use the AI
model; the others are ordinary program logic, which is fast and always gives the same answer. A
long RFP is cut into parts of about 1,800 words (roughly four pages) that overlap by 200 words, so a
sentence cut at a boundary is not lost. Each part is read separately, its facts are noted with page
numbers, and the notes from all parts are combined into one list for the summary step.

\section{Keeping the Document Safe}
The app only connects to programs on the same laptop: its own local server and the AI model runner.
The screen part of the app cannot read or write files by itself; it can only ask the main part of the
app for a small set of things, such as the server address, a file-picker dialog or a saved calendar
file. The PDF viewer is allowed to show only the RFP of the current analysis. The summary panel can
only ask the app to jump to a page number that exists in the document. One button, ``Google
Calendar'', opens a web page in the browser, and only when clicked; it sends that one event's title
and date to Google.

\section{The Seven Steps in Short}
Text is read from each page with pypdf and stored with its page number; contents pages are skipped.
The first attempt asked the model for everything in a part with one question. On five parts, two
answers were empty and one was cut off, so each part is now read twice: once for overview, scope,
requirements and eligibility, and once for dates, money, duration and legal conditions. Answers must
be in a fixed format with a page number for every point, and Pydantic checks the format. A slightly
malformed answer is repaired instead of thrown away. If the model fails on more than half of the
parts, the analysis is marked as failed. The facts are then grouped by heading and repeats are
removed. The issuing organization is the name found most often (names ending in Bank, Authority or
Ministry), and the answer is ``Not identified'' when Klaro is unsure. The summary step writes the nine
headings from the combined facts, and an empty heading is filled by copying its facts. Nine automatic
checks follow (structure, support by facts, page numbers, coverage, repeats, length, wrong heading
and others). They compare words, so they show whether the summary agrees with the extracted facts,
not whether the extraction was right.

\section{German Detection and Translation}
The language is guessed by counting common German and English words. If the text is too short or in
another language, the run stops and asks the user to choose the language. German text is cut into
segments (sentences and headings), and each segment is translated with the OPUS-MT German-English model
\cite{tiedemann2020opusmt}. The model runs offline; it is downloaded once during setup. Translated
segments are cached by a hash of their text, so a repeat run is faster. After translation, every number
in a German segment is looked for in its English segment, and a missing number sets a flag. Flags are
shown, never fixed by the program. If the model or a library is missing, the run fails at once with a
message that names the command that fixes it. Page numbers always stay the physical pages of the
original PDF.

\section{Source Highlighting}
The facts carry only a page number, and the summary step rewrites them, so there is no stored link from a
summary point to its source sentence. Klaro therefore builds the link after the summary is written. It
scores windows of one to three neighbouring segments on the cited page by word overlap with the point
(the first five letters of each word are compared) and keeps the best window if the score is 0.6 or
more. The quote, its German original and the score go into \texttt{evidence.json}. Below 0.6 no quote is
claimed, and the app says the exact passage was not found on that page. The viewer draws the PDF with
pdf.js and highlights whole text lines, not exact characters.

\section{English to German View}
On request, the finished summary is translated sentence by sentence with the OPUS-MT English-German
model. Only the text changes; headings, order, page numbers and the issuer name stay as they are.
Numbers, dates and currency signs are compared with the English text, and differences are flagged in a
separate file. The result is written to a staging folder and moved into place at the end, so a
cancelled translation leaves nothing behind.

\section{Calendar Export}
The dates are read from the ``Important dates'' bullets, and from other bullets only when they contain
an event word such as deadline, submission or opening (in English or German). Month names in both
languages, ISO dates, ranges and 12-hour or 24-hour times are understood. A numeric date such as
03/04/2026 is read day first and marked ``assumed'' when both numbers are 12 or less and differ. A date
with no year, no day or an impossible value goes to a ``needs review'' list. Klaro never guesses a year.
The user sees a preview and saves an \texttt{.ics} file, which any calendar program opens.

\section{Golden Dataset}
We collected 45 English RFPs from public sources in 8 sectors (2,583 pages in total, 21 to 111 pages
each, median 57). Of these, 35 are for training and 10 for testing, split by sector with a fixed
random seed. A written standard (nine sections, a page for every bullet, numbers copied exactly,
``Not specified in the document'' when absent) tells how each golden summary is written. 32 documents
have a summary so far: 22 for training and all 10 for testing. The summaries were drafted by an AI
model from the extracted text and passed automatic checks. They are therefore called
\emph{auto-checked}. \textbf{None has been checked by a human yet}, so they must not be called verified.
A guide for the human check (20 to 25 minutes per document) is in the repository.

\section{LoRA Fine-Tuning Kit}
The kit trains a LoRA adapter \cite{hu2022lora} on the golden summaries (training split only), merges it
into the model, loads it into Ollama and scores it on the 10 test documents. Training reads the training split only, and the scoring
script refuses a document that was used for training. Adapters are saved as safetensors only, and checkpoint files that use Python's pickle format are
deleted after a run. The kit was run only on a Mac with the 0.8-billion-parameter model of the same family:
the training loss fell from 1.62 to 0.20 over five epochs and the merged model loaded in Ollama.
\textbf{Nothing was trained or tested on the real 4-billion-parameter model.}

\textit{The 8 GB limit.} Our graphics card has 8 GB. A whole-summary example is too long for it: the
median document is 34,597 tokens, and only 1 of 32 is under 14,000. We estimate (plus or minus 30\%,
no GPU was used) that 4-bit training of the 4B model needs 14.7 GiB at 32,000 tokens and 7.8 GiB even
at 6,000. So we changed the design to \emph{per-section} examples: the pages one section cites, plus
that section's rules, as input, and that section's bullets as output, at most 4,096 tokens. This gives
322 training, 39 validation and 165 test examples, and we estimate 7.3 GiB for the 4B model (borderline
on 8 GB) and 5.2 GiB for a 2B fallback. The catch is that the app writes the whole summary in one call,
so this adapter cannot be used in the app yet (Chapter~7).

\section{The Context-Size Fix}\label{sec:ctxfix}
Ollama's default context here is 4,096 word pieces. The summary step sends all the combined facts in one
prompt. On the first test document the facts were 17,496 tokens, but the model saw 2,050: the rest was
cut off silently. The setting \texttt{KLARO\_NUM\_CTX} now lets the summary step ask for a larger
context. Its default is 0, which keeps the old behaviour, so the app only gets the fix when the
variable is set (we set it to 32768 for the new German run).

\section{One-Command Setup}
After \texttt{npm install}, the command \texttt{npm run dev} first runs a setup script. It finds
Python 3.11 or newer, creates a virtual environment, installs the pinned packages when the list
changed, downloads the two translation models (a failed English-German download only warns), and checks
Ollama. On a clean copy on a Mac the setup took 93 s, of which 74 s was the model download. It has not
been run on Windows.

\section{Testing}
68 JavaScript tests (54 of them for the calendar) and 43 Python tests pass. The checks include
the report pages, step order, failed parts, issuer detection, translation, highlighting and dates.
The app was driven by hand on macOS; the screenshots in this report were taken from the running app.
Automated tests do not drive the real window. A teammate is to test on Windows.

\subsection{Time Taken}
About 75\% to 95\% of the time goes into step 3, where the AI reads each part twice (G1: 446 s of 544 s;
G2: 564 s of 630 s; G3: 687 s of 921 s). Writing the summary takes one more minute (54 s in G1, 65 s in G2). An English 54 to
84 page RFP takes 22 to 25 minutes, and the shorter German RFPs 9 to 11 minutes. The progress screen
shows which part is being read.

\subsection{Same Document, Different Answers}
R2 and R3 were the same PDF, but the AI found 236 facts the first time and 243 the second. The model
does not give identical answers on every run, so improvements must be judged over several runs. Timings also
vary: step 3 took 446 s in G1 and 687 s in G3 on the same document, and the context setting does not touch that step.

\section{The Context-Size Experiment}
\textbf{Why long summaries were not shortened.} In every run above the summary is only 8\% to 10\% shorter
than the facts. For long RFPs, every point under the list headings was an exact copy of a fact. The
reason is in the numbers of Section~\ref{sec:ctxfix}: the summary step saw about 2,050 of 17,496 tokens, so its answer
was unusable for most headings, and the safety step copied every fact in. The high ``100\% backed by
facts'' score came partly from this copying.

We tested the fix on 11 golden documents with the same prompt and three settings of the context size
(Table~\ref{tab:ctx}). ``Within limits'' counts documents whose sections stay inside the limits of the
golden-summary standard.

\chapter{CONCLUSION AND FUTURE SCOPE}\nonum

\section{Conclusion}
Klaro works end to end on a laptop. An English RFP of 54 to 84 pages goes in, and 22 to 25 minutes later
a nine-heading summary comes out, with a page number on every point. A German RFP of 24 to 31 pages
takes 9 to 11 minutes, is translated on the laptop first, and each point can be traced to a highlighted
passage in the PDF. Nothing is sent over the internet. We chose Qwen3.5 because it made the fewest wrong
claims and is about a third of the size of Gemma. We also found why summaries of long RFPs were not
shortened, and a larger context setting reduces the problem at the price of a longer run. The fine-tuning
kit and the golden set are in place, but they have not yet improved the app.

\section{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item The golden summaries are drafted by an AI and checked by machine. No human has verified them.
\item The LoRA adapter has not been trained or tested on the real 4B model.
\item The per-section adapter is not wired into the app, because the app writes the whole summary in one call.
\item German translation quality has not been judged by a German speaker, and the translation A/B test has not been run.
\item The issuer line is wrong or partial in both German runs, and some points cite a page where the passage could not be found.
\item The context fix is off by default. With it only 6 of 11 summaries stay within the limits, the summary step is three to four times slower, and on one German run it kept only 65\% of the facts. Its first run on that document timed out at the default 300 s limit.
\item Only text-based PDFs work; tables lose their layout. Runs are slow (9 to 25 minutes).
\end{itemize}

\section{Future Scope}
\begin{itemize}
\item Full-length training on the university supercomputer next semester, with an evaluation on the 10 test documents.
\item Human verification of the golden set, starting with the 10 test documents.
\item A section-by-section summary step, so that the per-section adapter can be used in the app.
\item Scanned PDFs with text recognition, and Word files.
\item Two or three models for the user to choose from, so the user can pick the one that suits the laptop best. Later, more models can be packaged for download, and the user can use whichever fits the system specifications.
\item A multilingual Klaro: more languages beyond English and German, with the same offline translation and page checking.
\item A Proposal Assistant: a requirements checklist and a first proposal outline from the same reading of the RFP.
\item Human in the loop interaction.
\end{itemize}
